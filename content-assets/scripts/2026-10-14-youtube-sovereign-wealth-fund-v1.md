## 1:00 - WHAT IT IS, AND HOW IT STARTED
[Picture change: Abu Dhabi 1960s footage or stills]

First, what it actually is.

A sovereign wealth fund is a country's savings account. You sell something the world wants, you don't spend all the money, you invest the rest.

Abu Dhabi started pumping oil in 1962. Five years later, in 1967, the ruler set up a board to invest what was left over.

That's four years before the United Arab Emirates existed.

[On screen: OIL 1962 → FUND 1967 → COUNTRY 1971]

The fund is older than the country. And it's older than Norway's by more than twenty years.

Fifty years on, it's one point seven trillion dollars. Only two places on earth have saved more. China, with one point four billion people to share it between. And Norway, with five and a half million.

[On screen: 1. CHINA ~$3.5T  2. NORWAY $2.1T  3. ABU DHABI $1.7T]

Per person, Abu Dhabi has the biggest fund in the world. Around four hundred and fifteen thousand dollars for every resident of Abu Dhabi. Norway, the country everyone holds up as the example, is around three hundred and seventy.

So who's looking after all that money? This is where it gets interesting.

## 1:45 - THE THREE POTS, FAST
[Picture change each pot. Keep moving.]

Second, the structure.

It's not one fund. It's three, and they do three different jobs.

[On screen: three columns build as he goes]

ADIA. Around one point one trillion. Think of it as the pension. It sits in shares, bonds and property all over the world and it compounds. It doesn't build anything at home.

Mubadala. Three hundred and eighty-five billion. The investment account. The one that backs ideas - chips, AI, hospitals, and the company that builds most of Abu Dhabi.

L'imad. Around three hundred billion. The current account. It owns the things the city runs on - the airline, the airport, the railway, the power station, the hospitals.

Pension. Investment account. Current account. One point seven trillion between them.

## 7:00 - THE HONEST BIT
[Picture: Thames Water]

Thames Water.

ADIA bought ten per cent of it in 2011. In 2024 it wrote the whole thing off. Every pound. "The challenging regulatory environment", in its own words.

In 2007 it put seven and a half billion dollars into Citigroup. Citigroup lost ninety per cent of its value in two years.

So this isn't a fund that never loses. It's a fund so big it can lose a British water company and not notice.

And two more things I'd want to know if I were watching this.

One. Every number I've given you for ADIA is an estimate. It has never published its size. The one point seven trillion is the best public figure, not a filing.

Two. Government-owned isn't the protection. Dubai World was government-owned in 2009, and it couldn't pay a twenty-six billion dirham bill. The protection is the balance sheet behind the owner.

## 7:45 - 2009, AND WHY THIS MATTERS IF YOU BUY HERE
[Picture: Burj Khalifa opening night, 4 January 2010]

November 2009. Dubai's property boom had been built on borrowed money, and Dubai World asked its lenders for six months' grace on twenty-six billion dirhams of debt. Markets around the world dropped.

December the fourteenth. Abu Dhabi paid ten billion dollars to cover the bill. Around twenty billion all in, that year.

January the fourth, 2010. The tallest building in the world opened. It had been called the Burj Dubai for six years. That night it opened as the Burj Khalifa - named after the ruler of Abu Dhabi.

[On screen: BURJ DUBAI → BURJ KHALIFA, 4 JAN 2010]

Nobody has ever said that was the price. Everybody understood it.

That's what the fund is for.

Which brings me to the last one. Why any of this matters if you buy here.

So when you're buying off-plan here, this is the question that matters: who has to finish the building, and what's behind them?

In Abu Dhabi, the big developers are the funds. Modon is L'imad. Aldar is twenty-eight per cent Mubadala. The money that bailed out Dubai is the money behind the cranes.

And they build in a particular order. The wave pool, the velodrome and the beaches on Hudayriyat opened two years before the first villa hands over. Mubadala and Aldar announced a sixteen billion dollar expansion of the financial district - half a million square metres of offices, three thousand homes - before a single one is sold.

Infrastructure first. Homes second. You can only build in that order if you don't need the deposits to pay for the roads.
