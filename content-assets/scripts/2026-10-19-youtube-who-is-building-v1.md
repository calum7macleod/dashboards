SECOND, THE MONEY IN PLAIN ENGLISH

A sovereign wealth fund is a country's savings account. You sell something the world wants, you don't spend it all, you invest the rest.

Abu Dhabi started pumping oil in 1962. In 1967 - four years before the UAE existed - it started saving the profits. Nearly sixty years on, that's one point seven trillion dollars, in three pots that do three different jobs.
[On screen: OIL 1962 → FUND 1967 → COUNTRY 1971]

ADIA, around one point one trillion. The pension. It invests abroad and compounds - Gatwick Airport, a FTSE 100 company, around half a per cent of every listed company on earth. It doesn't build anything at home.
[On screen: ADIA ~$1.1TN - THE PENSION]

Mubadala, three hundred and eighty-five billion. The investment account. Chips, AI, and Aldar.
[On screen: MUBADALA $385BN - THE INVESTMENT ACCOUNT]

L'imad, around three hundred billion. The current account. It owns the things the city runs on - the airline, the airport, the railway, the power company, the hospitals - and Modon.
[On screen: L'IMAD ~$300BN - THE CURRENT ACCOUNT]

Here's why that matters for a half-built island. The developer doesn't need your deposit to pay for the roads. Hudayriyat opened the world's largest surf pool, a velodrome and sixteen kilometres of beach before a single villa handed over. The Maryah expansion is putting the bridges in before the homes. You can only build in that order when the owner has the money already.
[On screen: Surf Abu Dhabi, velodrome, Marsana - "open before handover"]

That's the money. Now the honest part - because government-owned has gone wrong before, twice.

THIRD, THE TWO TIMES IT WENT WRONG

November 2009. Dubai's state companies had borrowed heavily, and Dubai World - government-owned - asked its lenders for six months' grace on twenty-six billion dollars of debt. Markets around the world dropped.
[On screen: "26 November 2009 - Dubai World, $26bn standstill"]

December the fourteenth, 2009. Abu Dhabi provided ten billion dollars. Around twenty billion all in, that year.
[On screen: "14 December 2009 - Abu Dhabi, $10bn"]

January the fourth, 2010. The tallest building in the world opened. It had been the Burj Dubai for six years. That night it opened as the Burj Khalifa - named after Abu Dhabi's ruler, the President of the UAE, as a thank you.
[On screen: BURJ DUBAI → BURJ KHALIFA, 4 January 2010]

Then 2011, closer to home. Abu Dhabi's own property market had fallen hard after the crash. Aldar - the government-backed developer - needed support. The government stepped in with around sixteen billion dirhams.
[On screen: "2011 - Aldar, ~AED 16bn government support"]

Two things to take from those. One: government-owned is not the protection. Dubai World was government-owned, and it still had to ask for time. The protection is the balance sheet behind the owner - and in 2009 and 2011, the balance sheet showed up.

Two: the floor is under the developer, not under your price. Abu Dhabi prices fell in 2009 and took years to recover. The fund didn't stop that. What it did was make sure the buildings got finished.
[On screen: "Floor under the developer. Not under the price."]

And the funds lose money too. ADIA bought ten per cent of Thames Water in 2011 and wrote the whole stake off in 2024. It's a fund so big it can lose a British water company and not feel it - but it can lose.
[On screen: Thames Water - "written off, 2024"]

FOURTH, WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOUR DEPOSIT

Three layers between you and the thing going wrong.

The first is the law. In Abu Dhabi, off-plan payments go into an escrow account the regulator controls, released to the developer as construction milestones are hit. The developer can't spend your deposit on something else.
[On screen: "Escrow - regulator-controlled, released on milestones"]

The second is the developer. Modon and Aldar are the two biggest, and both are majority or substantially owned by the sovereign funds. That's not a guarantee - nothing is - but it's the difference between a company that needs your money to build and one that doesn't.

The third is the pattern. Infrastructure first, homes second. If the roads, the beaches and the schools are going in before the villas are sold, the money isn't coming from your deposit.

Is off-plan safe here? Safer than most places, for those three reasons. Not risk-free. Prices can fall - they did in 2009. Handover dates slip - Hudayriyat's first villas were due at the end of 2026. And a developer that isn't one of those two is a different conversation.
